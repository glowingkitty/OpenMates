---
title: Press release structure and what journalists need
description: How a press release is built, what makes it usable for journalists, and the mistakes that get it ignored.
when_to_use: Writing, reviewing, shortening or translating a press release or media announcement, setting up a press style, or writing a short pitch email to journalists.
---
Use this as working guidance, not as rigid rules. A company's own press style and
the user's explicit wishes take priority. Say so when a choice is a convention
rather than a requirement.

What a journalist needs
- A real news hook: something new, specific and relevant to the outlet's readers
  now (a launch, a number, a partnership, a funding round, a study, an event, a
  change). If there is no hook, say so honestly and suggest what would make one,
  instead of dressing up a non-event.
- The essentials in the first paragraph, so the release still works if only the
  headline and first paragraph are read.
- Facts the journalist can check and quote, a named person to quote, and someone
  to contact who actually answers.

Structure, top to bottom
- Optional label line: "PRESS RELEASE" or "Pressemitteilung", and either
  "For immediate release" or an embargo line with exact date, time and time zone
  ("Embargoed until 18 November 2026, 09:00 CET").
- Headline: one line, about 6 to 12 words, active verb, concrete news, no
  question, no pun that hides the news, no all-caps.
- Optional subheadline: one sentence that adds the second most important fact.
- Dateline at the start of the first paragraph: CITY, date – (for example
  "Berlin, 18 November 2026 –"). Use the place where the news happens or the
  company is based.
- Lead paragraph: who, what, when, where and why in two or three sentences.
- Body in inverted pyramid order: most important details first, background and
  context later. Short paragraphs of two to four sentences.
- One or two quotes from named people with their role. A quote adds opinion,
  motivation or vision; it does not repeat facts from the text. It should sound
  like a person speaking, not like marketing copy. Quotes need the approval of
  the person quoted before publication.
- Availability or next steps where relevant: date, price, where to get it, link.
- Boilerplate: "About [company]" in three to five factual sentences (what the
  company does, for whom, since when, where, one proof point), the same in every
  release.
- Media contact: name, role, email and phone that are monitored, and a link to
  a press kit or images if there is one.
- End mark: "###" or "-30-" in English; in German releases often omitted or
  replaced by the boilerplate and contact block.

Length and language
- About 300 to 500 words for most news; one page is a good target, two at most.
- Plain, specific, factual. Third person for the company ("OpenSun launches"),
  never "we" outside quotes.
- Avoid empty superlatives and buzzwords: "revolutionary", "game-changing",
  "world-leading", "unique", "innovative", "next-generation", "excited to
  announce", "seamless". Show the claim with a fact or remove it.
- Numbers as numbers, with source and date for any statistic. Explain
  abbreviations on first use.
- English: AP style is the common default (dates like "Nov. 18, 2026", numerals
  for 10 and above). German: Duden spelling, full date "18. November 2026",
  formal and neutral tone. Follow the press style if it says otherwise.

Formats
- Editable document for review and approval is the default. A PDF is used for
  sending and archiving. An HTML version suits an online newsroom page; keep the
  same text and add the structured parts (headline, date, contact) as real
  headings and elements.
- Links instead of attachments in emails; images in a press kit with captions
  and credits.

Pitch email to journalists (separate from the release)
- Personal, short (about 100 to 150 words), sent to one journalist at a time.
- Subject line states the news, not "Press release".
- Why it matters to this outlet's readers, the one key fact, what is available
  (interview, data, images, exclusive or embargoed access), and the release
  pasted below or linked. No mass-mail wording.

Common mistakes
- Burying the news under company history.
- Invented or unapproved quotes, unchecked numbers, claims without a source.
- Missing date, place or contact; a contact who does not answer.
- Wrong or inconsistent spelling of company, product and people names.
- Embargo without a time zone, or embargoed news posted publicly early.
- Sending to large lists without personalisation.
